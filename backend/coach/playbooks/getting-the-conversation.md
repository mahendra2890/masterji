# Playbook: Getting the Conversation
*(inspired by Giff Constable's "Talking to Humans" — Masterji's own distillation, not the book)*

## What this covers, and what it doesn't
The customer-conversations playbook is what to say once you are in the room.
This is how you get into the room. Nobody is waiting for your message, nobody
owes you ten minutes, and the gap between the route you already wrote and the
first real note is where most builders quietly stop.

## Ask for advice, never pitch
A student asking to learn gets a yes where a founder pitching gets silence.
You are not selling anything — you do not yet know enough to sell it, and
saying so is both true and disarming. "I'm trying to understand how this
actually works for people like you" costs the other person nothing to be
generous about, and it is the only frame under which a stranger will tell you
something unflattering. Put your idea in the first message and you have turned
a favour into a transaction nobody agreed to.

## The first message, line by line
Four lines. Nothing else:
1. **Who you are, one clause.** "I'm a second-year at ___." Not a title you
   invented for the message.
2. **Why them, specifically.** You already wrote this part: your route says
   why you believe these people are there. Say it back to them. "I saw your
   post in the sellers' group about returns eating your margin." A message
   that could have gone to anyone gets answered by nobody.
3. **Ten minutes, with a day attached.** "Could I ask you about it for ten
   minutes — Thursday evening?" A named length and a named day is a decision
   they can make now. "Sometime?" is a decision they can postpone forever.
4. **No link, no deck, no idea.** Nothing to evaluate means nothing to
   politely decline.

## Silence is arithmetic
Send five to get one. That is not a bad rate, that is the rate. A no-reply is
not a verdict on the problem — it is a person who was busy on a Tuesday.
Builders who send one message, hear nothing, and conclude the problem isn't
real have tested nothing at all. Count what you sent, not how it felt: five
out tonight, one booked by the weekend.

## Standing in the room beats messaging it
Your route named a place. If that place has an address — the shop, the clinic,
the mess, the market lane — go and stand in it. Ten minutes in person is
easier to get than ten minutes over text, because ignoring a message is free
and ignoring a person in front of you is not.

## End every conversation with the ladder
"Who else has this — can you introduce me?" Ask it every time, while they are
still warm. One yes becomes three conversations, and an intro is itself a
commitment, which is the part of the bar people find hardest to get.

## What counts as PROOF for Masterji
Nothing changes here: the proof is still the conversation note, and
customer-conversations says what has to be in it. Messages sent are not proof.
A booked meeting is not proof. The conversation happened, or it did not.

## Signals you're hiding
- Rewriting the outreach message for the third evening instead of sending it.
- Waiting for a reply before sending the next four.
- "I'll message them once the product looks more legitimate."
- Picking the room where you know somebody over the room where the problem is.
