# Playbook: Narrowing the First User
*(inspired by Geoffrey Moore's beachhead segment in "Crossing the Chasm" — Masterji's own distillation)*

## "Be specific" is advice you can agree with and still not act on
You have already been told the "who" has to be specific. That tells you to cut
and not where, so the usual result is a sentence with more adjectives in it and
the same guess underneath: "college students" becomes "tech-savvy urban college
students", which is the same people wearing a description. And you resist the
cut anyway, because every one of them looks like market you are throwing away.

Both problems have the same answer, and it is about which axis you cut on.

## Cut on circumstance, not on attribute
An attribute describes a person: age, city, course, "small business". A
circumstance describes a moment — what happened, when, and how often it happens
again. "Small businesses" is an attribute. "Kirana shops that write credit in a
notebook and spend the last two days of the month chasing it" is a
circumstance, and it is the one with an address.

One question tests it: could somebody match your description perfectly and not
have the problem? If yes, you cut on an attribute and your "who" is a filter,
not a group. Circumstance is also what gathers people — the same situation puts
them in the same places — which is why cutting this way hands you a room
instead of leaving you to go looking for one.

## The Thursday test
Two questions, and both are answered at your desk. Name the room. Then: could
you be standing in it this Thursday?

Could, not will — and the difference is the whole rule. You are not going on
Thursday and you are not messaging anyone; that is VALIDATION's work and it
starts the moment this phase unlocks. What the question tests is whether a door
exists at all. A room you have heard of but cannot enter this week is not yours
yet: the paid community you are not in, the conference in November, the trade
you know nobody in. If the honest answer is "I'd have to go find one first",
the "who" is still a category, and cutting it again is tonight's work.

## They have to be able to hear each other
A group whose members have never met is a list, not a segment. Ten people with
the same problem and no contact between them are ten separate first users, and
nothing you learn from one of them travels to the next. A group that compares
notes — the resellers who forward each other leads, the night shift on one
ward — carries your second user to you for the price of the first.

## Narrowing widens the route
This is the part that reads backwards. The market on your slide gets smaller
and the route to it gets bigger, and the route is the thing you are short of. A
"who" narrow enough to have an address answers "who else?" by itself: the
second person is in the same room as the first. A wide one has no adjacency at
all — everybody, therefore nobody in particular, therefore advertising.

The wide market does not go anywhere. It is reached through the narrow one
rather than instead of it, and you can be the obvious answer for two hundred
people in a way nobody is ever the obvious answer for two hundred million.

## Signals you're hiding
- Adding adjectives instead of conditions: the sentence got longer and the
  group did not get smaller.
- Widening the "who" again the moment it starts to feel too small to bother
  with.
- Naming a room and then describing your users as everyone that room might
  eventually lead to.
- "I'll narrow it once I see who actually signs up."
