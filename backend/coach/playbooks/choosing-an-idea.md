# Playbook: Choosing the One Idea
*(inspired by Paul Graham's "How to Get Startup Ideas" and Dalton Caldwell's YC talks on tarpit ideas — Masterji's own distillation)*

## Why choosing is real work
One goal at a time is a rule the server keeps, which makes the choice of goal
the most leveraged decision you make here. It is also the decision people
spend three weeks not making, because collecting ideas feels like progress and
picking one feels like losing the other two.

## Noticed beats invented
The ideas worth a semester are the ones you noticed, not the ones you
generated. A problem you have personally stood in front of — a queue, a bill,
a form you filled in twice, a workaround you watched somebody run — arrives
with its people attached, because you know where you were standing when it
happened. An idea produced in a brainstorm arrives with nothing attached and
usually stays that way.

## The tarpits
A few ideas attract every first-time builder on every campus: food delivery
for the hostel, notes and PYQ sharing, event discovery, a marketplace for
seniors' used things. They are tarpits, not because the problem is fake — the
problem is real, which is exactly what makes them sticky — but because what
stops them is never the code. It is supply, or trust, or the fact that a free
WhatsApp group already does eighty percent of it.

If yours is on that list you may still build it, and this is not a refusal.
What you owe yourself is the question you have been avoiding: somebody on this
campus tried this last year — what actually stopped them? Hold the question,
don't go asking it tonight. Finding out is VALIDATION's work and it starts the
moment this phase unlocks. But if you cannot imagine any answer at all, you
are choosing the tarpit because it is familiar, not because it is yours.

## The "I'd use it" trap
"Well, I would use it" is the sentence that keeps a weak idea alive. Are you
actually in the queue — did you personally hit this in the last month — or
does it merely sound plausible when you say it out loud? Being a user is
evidence. Being able to imagine a user is not.

## Schlep blindness
The boring idea with a findable user beats the impressive one without. Work
that looks tedious from the outside — chasing shopkeepers, reconciling
spreadsheets, doing by hand what should be automatic — is left alone by
everyone else for that exact reason, which is why there is room in it. The
idea that sounds best when you describe it to a placement panel is usually the
one with the most competitors and the fewest reachable users.

## The tiebreaker: whose room can you walk into?
When two ideas are close, the one whose people you could reach this week wins.
Not the bigger market, not the one you're more excited about — the one with a
room. Excitement is not a differentiator; every idea is exciting on the
evening you have it. A route is a differentiator, and it is the thing the next
piece of work will demand of you anyway.

## What counts as a chosen idea
Four things, and they are exactly the four this phase will ask you to write
down: the problem in a paragraph — who has it, what they do about it today,
and why that is bad; one specific place these people already are; why you
believe they're there; and how you'd get one conversation this week. If you
can answer those for one idea and not for the others, the choice has already
been made and you are only postponing the writing.

## Switching is cheap, and it stays cheap
Closing a goal is free and the record survives it. A goal you are keeping out
of guilt is worth less than the one you would choose today. So commit fully to
one and write the other down somewhere that isn't here — a second idea kept
warm on the side is a way of not testing the first.

## Signals you're hiding
- Collecting a fourth idea instead of testing the first.
- Ranking ideas by how they sound in a placement interview.
- "I'll pick once I've done a bit more research."
- Picking the idea with the best name, or the one your friend liked.
- Waiting to feel certain. Nobody starts certain; they start with a route.
