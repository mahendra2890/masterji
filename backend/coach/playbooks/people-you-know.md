# Playbook: Talking to People You Know
*(inspired by Rob Fitzpatrick's "The Mom Test" — its title is about exactly this bias — Masterji's own distillation, not the book)*

## What this covers, and what it doesn't
Customer-conversations gives you the three rules; getting-the-conversation gets
you into a room with a stranger. This is the room you were always going to start
in: friends, classmates, flatmates, the senior who owes you a favour. They say
yes when nobody else does, which is exactly why the first three conversations of
VALIDATION are the hardest to read honestly.

The gate counts **people**, not evenings. Who your first three are is
load-bearing.

## The friend who says yes teaches you nothing
Somebody who wants you to succeed is answering a different question from the one
you asked. You say "would you use this?" and they hear "do you want me to be
encouraging?" — and they are decent, so they say yes. You have learned that they
like you. You knew that.

The trap is that it feels like progress. Three warm yeses in one evening looks
like a validated problem and is closer to an empty notebook, because none of it
came from anybody's behaviour.

## What a friend is actually good for
Do not stop talking to them. Point them at what they can genuinely give:

- **The introduction.** The real prize. "Who do you know who has this worse than
  you?" turns one friend into two strangers, and a trusted intro opens a door a
  cold message will not.
- **Their own history, not their opinion.** They have a past. Ask about it.
- **The rehearsal.** Your first conversation will be clumsy. Better it be clumsy
  with your flatmate than with the one shop owner who was going to talk to you.

## The framing that gets them talking about their life
Take your idea out of the room. That single change does most of the work.

- **Don't say what you're building.** Not the name, not the pitch, not "so I
  have this idea". Once it is on the table they are reviewing you, and
  everything after is a compliment or a hedge.
- **Ask about last time, not next time.** "When did you last miss dinner
  because the mess shut?" is a Tuesday they can describe. "Would you use an
  app that…" is free.
- **Ask for something small and real.** Money, an hour, an intro, their order
  history. What a friend will *not* give up is the most honest signal you will
  get from them.
- **If they start advising you on the product, you have lost the frame.** Bring
  it back to their own last time.

## When to stop mining your own circle
Your friends are your age, on your campus, with your timetable — a problem all
of them share might be a problem only your corridor has.

Never all three from one room: three flatmates is one data point wearing three
shirts. And after two conversations with people you know, spend the next on
somebody who has no reason to be nice to you. You will find out immediately how
much of the warmth was the problem and how much was the friendship.

## What counts as PROOF for Masterji
The bar does not soften for a friend, and it does not harden either: who you
spoke to, three things they said in their own words, what they last actually did
about the problem, and the commitment you asked for and whether you got it.

The gate counts **distinct people**, so three nights of notes about the same
hostelmate are three honest days of work and one person's word. VALIDATION
exists to establish that more than one person has this problem, and one person
cannot establish it however well you know them.

## Signals you're hiding
- All three of your people eat in the same mess as you.
- You said the idea out loud in the first minute, every time.
- Your notes are full of "he said it's a good idea" and empty of what he did
  last Tuesday.
- You have not asked one person for an introduction.
- You are picking the next conversation by who is easiest to reach.
